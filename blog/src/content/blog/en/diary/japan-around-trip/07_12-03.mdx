## Journal  
  
### Fukui  
  
Today, I woke up around 7 o'clock, checked out, and moved to Fukui Station to go to Eiheiji.  
I skipped breakfast because I wasn’t hungry because I ate a lot yesterday.  
I found an izakaya on the way, and although they said it was a limited time service (before 7pm on weekdays), they sold highballs and lemon sours for 80 yen...  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250108015522725.webp" alt="image-20250108015522725" />  
  
I was thinking of buying a 1,800 yen a day bus pass at Echizen Railway Fukui Station, but today's schedule was to go to Fukui until around 2pm, so I decided to just use the IC card.  
Echizen Railway's Fukui Station looked newly renovated, but the one-person-operated train itself seemed quite old.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250108015910399.webp" alt="image-20250108015910399" />  
  
When I arrived at Eiheijiguchi on the Echizen Railway, I was greeted by the local character ''Katsuyama Teira (勝山ていら)''.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250108020143507.webp" alt="image-20250108020143507" />  
  
There was about 20 minutes left before the bus to Eiheiji, so I walked to the bus stop a little further ahead and caught the bus. Saved about 80 yen...  
  
And so I arrived at the Eiheiji bus stop.  
Because it is the main temple of the Soto sect, it is large in size, so there are many shopping streets targeting tourists heading to Eiheiji.  
Mainly soba shops and souvenir shops  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250108020319143.webp" alt="image-20250108020319143" />

I left the shopping street behind and walked straight toward Eiheiji.  
As soon as I arrived, the scenery changed dramatically. The sight of moss and tall coniferous trees made me feel like I was in another world.  
It was December 3rd, and the autumn leaves were still there, and other than the temple, various Buddha statues, sculptures, roads, bridges, etc. were covered in moss, but it wasn't dirty at all and the mysterious feeling was much stronger.

The scenery was so beautiful that I took pictures and admired it before entering the building inside Eiheiji.  
Admission fee is 700 yen.  
The buildings inside were not temples similar to the shrines commonly seen in Japan, and if I had to be specific, I thought they were similar to temples in Korea.  
As a head temple of the Soto school, the complex had many large buildings. The route through them was quite long.  
  
The Soto sect is a sect of Buddhism founded by Zen master Dogen and is said to have begun with the founding of Eiheiji in 1244.  
The training method is called zazen, and it is said that in the space of one tatami, looking at the wall, maintaining a consistently correct posture and mind, trying to get rid of all kinds of delusions and desires and reach Buddha.  
There are also several audio-visual materials explaining Eiheiji and the Soto sect within the temple, so I was able to learn more about this sect.

The interior of the building was similar to a temple in Korea, but there were subtle differences.  
The layout and composition of the building did not feel similar to the shrines commonly seen in Japan, but rather, I thought it was similar to temples in Korea.  
The Great Buddha Hall and the main hall were richly decorated with gold, and the floor was covered with tatami mats, giving it a refreshing feel.

An advertisement for Chitose Is in the Ramune Bottle, spotted at Eiheiji too.  
After going through all the circuits, it took about an hour.  
As a souvenir, I bought an amulet in the shape of flip-flops (an amulet to pray for healthy legs and feet...)  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250108021748958.webp" alt="image-20250108021748958" />

<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250108021949163.webp" alt="image-20250108021949163" />

Buddhist statues stood among moss-covered surroundings untouched by people. It felt as though I'd traveled back to the Warring States period, like Kagome in Inuyasha.  
I think the combination of sunlight coming through the trees is a truly memorable sight.

After looking around for a bit, I found a sign that said "15-minute walk to Atago Kannon Hall" and decided to go.  
The road was a gentle hill, and there were sections with stairs.  
However, there was a lot of moss, and the leaves were unorganized and moist, so the path was very slippery and I almost fell a few times.

I forgot to photograph the sesame tofu zenzai. It was a light red-bean soup with sesame tofu in it, and quite good.  
The local sake was decent, but the tororo soba and oroshi soba were a little disappointing.  
I tried it because it was Echizen's specialty, but I felt it didn't suit me... Still, the noodles were chewy and tasty.  
A total of 2,550 yen came out.

### Kanazawa  
  
I took the Shinkansen from Fukui Station and arrived in Kanazawa a little after 3 o'clock.  
I had a little trouble with the jr pass. Once I'd reserved a route for that day, I couldn't make another reservation for the same route at a different time.  
For example, if you reserved a seat on the 12:40 departure and missed that Shinkansen, you couldn't reserve another seat.  
I'd need to check whether that restriction applied only within a certain time window.  
I thought it was completely unlimited, but it's not, so I think you need to be careful when using it.  
(There are quite a few trains on the Hokuriku Shinkansen where all seats are reserved, so you may have to pay extra...)  
  
First, I stopped by the tourist information center and picked up a few pamphlets for tourist reference. As I looked around, another character I didn't know greeted me.  
It seemed to be promotional material for the animated film A Few Moments of Cheers, which is set in Ishikawa Prefecture.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250108024810050.webp" alt="image-20250108024810050" />  
  
These characters promote Hokuriku's three conventional railway lines.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250108024828710.webp" alt="image-20250108024828710" />  
  
As I came out of the station, the magnificent architecture of Kanazawa Station captivated me.  
Tsuzumimon Gate and Motenashi Dome.  
It was selected as one of the most beautiful train stations in the world by an American travel magazine.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250108024647373.webp" alt="image-20250108024647373" />  
  
It took about 10 minutes to get to the accommodation by bus.  
The hotel was Amanek Kanazawa. I booked three nights because I planned to explore both Kanazawa and Toyama.  
For a 3-star hotel, it was quite clean and had quite a few facilities. (Small gym, washing machine, welcome drink zone, etc.)  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250108030044830.webp" alt="image-20250108030044830" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250108025838377.webp" alt="image-20250108025838377" />  
  
The room was neat and nice, but most of all, it had a smart TV , so I could log in to my YouTube account and watch YouTube on a wide screen.  
However, the downside is that in case of consecutive nights, room cleaning was not possible and only amenities could be replenished.  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250108030141790.webp" alt="image-20250108030141790" />  
  
After resting at the hotel for a while, I went outside and the sun had already set.  
At night, sightseeing options were limited, so I first explored Nagamachi, the former samurai residential district.  
The evening scenery after the rain was impressive, but the streetlights were so strong that the pictures didn't come out well...  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250108030442736.webp" alt="image-20250108030442736" />  
  
Uniquely, the walls are lined with straw, and the trees are supported with twine. This is said to be done in this way to protect the trees and walls because Kanazawa is an area that receives a lot of snow.  
Nagamachi was lovely at night, but I wanted to see it in daylight too, so I decided to return the next morning.

There was this romantic scenery along the way, so I took a picture.

Oyama Shrine has a unique building that is not suitable for a shrine. It is a gate with stained glass that was completed in 1875 and is said to have originally served as a lighthouse.  
The combination of a shrine and stained glass seems to create a rare scenery. Harmony of East and West.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250108031048813.webp" alt="image-20250108031048813" />  
  
When you come to a shrine, you cannot miss out on worship and omikuji.  
I chose a love fortune, a koimikuji. It featured an origami girl tied with a red thread, the symbol of a romantic connection. My result was good luck. Lately, I seemed to get only small luck or good luck, which felt a little disappointing.  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250108031311533.webp" alt="image-20250108031311533" />  
  
There is a garden behind the shrine, so I took a walk. The view of the garden at night was nice, so I took some pictures.

After looking around the shrine, I headed off to dinner.  
On the way, I took a picture of the central area of ​​Kitamachi. It feels quite bustling. <ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250108031514279.webp" alt="image-20250108031514279" />  
  
Tonight's destination was an oden restaurant called Miyuki. Kanazawa is known for oden and crab, and I chose oden.  
The store called Miyuki was quite famous in the area, so there was a bit of a wait. About 10 people in front of me.  
Since it was also a bar, I thought I might wait an hour. But tables turned over quickly, and I got a counter seat after about 20 minutes.  
  
The store seemed to be visited mainly by locals, but seeing as it also has foreign language menus, it seems tourists also come here often.  
Besides the owner and his wife, there seemed to be about four part-timers. Even so, a notice said they were short-staffed. I couldn't imagine how busy it must get on the peak days, Friday through Sunday...  
The store was quite large and could accommodate about 30 customers.  
Perhaps because it is oden, the food came out quickly and the turnover rate was high. So it seemed like I didn't have to wait as long as I thought. <ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250108031832837.webp" alt="image-20250108031832837" />  
  
I started with a mega mug of draft beer. The menu said it was twice the size of a medium mug, but it felt more like 2.5 times. About 1.5 L? I regretted it a little.  
They served lots of local sake too, but with that much beer to finish, I had to give it a miss.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250108031854572.webp" alt="image-20250108031854572" />  
  
There were many different types of oden to eat, but I ordered one assorted dish first.  
It included red-rolled kamaboko fish cake, squid balls, daikon, rolled omelet, sea snail, seaweed, fukashi fish cake, and kurumafu wheat gluten.  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250108032222857.webp" alt="image-20250108032222857" />  
  
I think the combination of beer and dashi-infused oden is the best.  
The seaweed and sea snail weren't really my favorites, so for round two I chose the oden ingredients I liked.  
My second plate had shumai dumplings, chikuwa fish cake, chicken meatballs, shirataki noodles, and fried tofu. All delicious.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250108032301389.webp" alt="image-20250108032301389" />  
  
I also ordered tororo teppanyaki, which was marked as a recommended dish.  
A combination of grated yam and cheese. It's not bad, but maybe it's because I don't like yam very much.  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250108032401466.webp" alt="image-20250108032401466" />  
  
I'd planned to eat a modest amount of oden, then finish at a ramen shop I'd had my eye on. But I was much too full, so I bought a few snacks at a convenience store and went back to the hotel instead.  
Back at the hotel, I tried the Kokuryu Daiginjo with FamilyMart chicken and snacks.  
Kokuryu Daiginjo is not a bad liquor with a moderate rice taste. It didn't seem to go well with Family Chicken.  
  
Oshi no Ko snacks and Bocchi the Rock! candy, bought from the convenience store's clearance section.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250108032503300.webp" alt="image-20250108032503300" />
