## Travel Diary  
  
Since breakfast was included in the accommodation I booked, I started my day with breakfast at the inn.  
Various kinds of pickled side dishes, hoba miso steak, grilled saury in soy sauce, etc.  
The meal was great. If I had known it was this delicious, I would have ordered it for dinner...  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221182406831.webp" alt="image-20250221182406831" />  
  
After eating breakfast and checking out, I went to the bus terminal to go to my next destination, Matsumoto.  
Since it's Hida Takayama, you can't miss Hyouka.  
I heard there was an episode in episode 7 of the anime where they visited one of Hirayu's ryokans, but I don't remember.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221182616773.webp" alt="image-20250221182616773" />  
  
Anyway, I arrived at Matsumoto by bus around 10:20. I think it took about 2 hours.  
The bus fare is 2800 yen.  
First, I had to leave my heavy luggage, so I went to the accommodation.  
The accommodation is Hotel M Matsumoto, an inexpensive capsule hotel.  
On the third floor of the building, there is a store that sells subculture goods called Surugaya.  
Unusually, the Korean translation was well done.  
Matsumoto City may not be a well-known tourist destination for Koreans... I was even surprised by the good translation quality.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221183019303.webp" alt="image-20250221183019303" />  
  
I left my luggage at the hotel and walked towards Matsumoto Castle, a famous spot in Matsumoto City. (It's an awkward distance to take the bus...)  
  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221183740537.webp" alt="image-20250221183740537" />  
  
While walking down the street, the first stop was the Nawate Street shopping district.  
It was a small shopping street below the castle, and not many places were open that day.  
After looking around briefly, I left the shopping street.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221183815460.webp" alt="image-20250221183815460" />  
  
The next place I visited was Yohashira Shrine.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221183944934.webp" alt="image-20250221183944934" />  
  
It is said that the name Yohashira came from the fact that it worships four main gods.  
Among them was Amaterasu Omikami.  
Founded in 1879, it does not have a long history for a shrine.  
Still, there were quite a few people on the grounds, perhaps because the shrine is famous as a power spot in the region.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221183958569.webp" alt="image-20250221183958569" />  
  
I could leave an enmusubi negai-gushi prayer stick for free, so I tried writing one.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221184045345.webp" alt="image-20250221184045345" />  
  
Unusually, there was also an Enmusubi pine tree.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221184635822.webp" alt="image-20250221184635822" />  
  
Before going to Matsumoto Castle, I also visited the tourist information center.  
As shown in the photo below, the tourist course is decorated beautifully and cutely.  
I couldn't follow the suggested route because I planned to head to Suwa after visiting the castle...  
Since I received a Shinshu soba map here, I thought about going to one of the restaurants on the list for lunch.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221184720445.webp" alt="image-20250221184720445" />  
  
After some trouble, I arrived at Matsumoto Castle.  
A volunteer guide was waiting by the entrance. When I stopped to take a look, he asked if I would like a tour of Matsumoto Castle. I gladly accepted.  
  
The guide was Nakazawa-san. When I told him I was from Korea, he said he had visited Korea quite often during his working years, when he did business with companies called LS and Daedong.  
My family farms, so LS and Daedong were familiar names. That gave me an unexpected sense of connection...  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221225955367.webp" alt="image-20250221225955367" />  
  
Touring the castle while listening to the history and anecdotes about Matsumoto Castle starting from the Warring States Period was a different level of fun than exploring it alone.  
Among them, the most impressive anecdote is that Matsumoto Castle was in danger of being demolished due to the decree of abolishing the castle after the Meiji Restoration, but the demolition of the castle was prevented by local influential figures, including Ryozo Ichigawa.  
The Meiji government had intended to sell Matsumoto Castle and its grounds for the equivalent of about 20 million Korean won today. A campaign to raise donations, buy the castle, and preserve it allowed it to survive as cultural heritage.  
I thought it was a very interesting piece of history that such a huge and beautiful castle was almost sold for only 20 million won.  
  
He then told me about a hidden viewpoint: the city hall rooftop near Matsumoto Castle is open to the public and offers a good view of the castle. I headed straight there and was able to see it from a relatively high vantage point.  
It was a bit disappointing that there were clouds, but the Japanese Alps could also be seen in the distance.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221231007585.webp" alt="image-20250221231007585" />  
  
Next, he introduced me to several springs in Matsumoto City. Did he say that this was the first spring, or that it was selected as one of the top 100 springs? I don't remember.  
In addition to this place, Matsumoto has several springs, so traveling to these places seemed like it would be quite fun.  
Does the water taste just normal? I don't know what the big difference is from regular bottled water.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221231230069.webp" alt="image-20250221231230069" />  
  
Matsumoto Castle surrounded by a moat.  
According to Nakazawa-san, it was possible to reach the castle tower through the red bridge seen on the left, but it was damaged due to the earthquake and was closed due to danger after repair.  
I wanted to cross it, but it was a bit disappointing.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221231212221.webp" alt="image-20250221231212221" />  
  
Next, a tour of the inside of the castle tower.  
I parted ways with Nakazawa-san here, as he wasn't able to accompany me inside the castle...  
  
Entry to the castle itself is free, but there is an entrance fee of 700 yen to enter the castle tower.  
Like other Japanese castles, the inside of the castle tower was created as a museum consisting of relics from the existing castle.  
Since it is a castle deeply connected to the Warring States Period, you cannot miss Sekigahara Battle.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221231301834.webp" alt="image-20250221231301834" />  
  
A view taken through a narrow window from the top of the castle tower.  
The day was very cloudy and the scenery was a bit disappointing.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221232102671.webp" alt="image-20250221232102671" />  
  
When I came out of the castle tower, there was a staff member in a ninja costume at the exit, posing in various poses and catching the children's attention.  
The pose was so cool that I asked for a video and took some.  
The video doesn't quite do it justice; in person, his movements were much sharper and more impressive.  
  
<VideoLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/20241211_040728000_iOS.MOV" />  
  
On the way out, there was a sign listing the past lords of Matsumoto Castle.  
From the famous Kazumasa Ishikawa to the Matsudaira clan of the Tokugawa family.  
I noticed the kokudaka, the domain's assessed rice yield, falling from 80,000 to 60,000 koku, and wondered why.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221232548775.webp" alt="image-20250221232548775" />  
  
After touring Matsudaira Castle, it was now time for lunch, so I decided to visit Daikita, one of the Shinshu soba restaurants that I received from the tourist information center that was near the castle and had good Google reviews.  
Although it had only recently opened, the store had the feel of a clean and organized Japanese restaurant.  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221233016542.webp" alt="image-20250221233016542" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221233515826.webp" alt="image-20250221233515826" />  
  
The menu consisted of a course meal, and since it was lunch, I ordered the 3,000 yen course.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221232923985.webp" alt="image-20250221232923985" />  
  
As the name of the store, 酒と蕎麦大北, suggests, you can try a variety of local sakes.  
Sake was organized by type so it was easy to choose.  
Shinshu Kirei, Daishinshu, Mikotsuru, Kame no Umi, and more—all highly rated on saketime.  
There were so many varieties that choosing what to drink was difficult.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221233348487.webp" alt="image-20250221233348487" />  
  
After some thought, I chose Mikotsuru Junmai Ginjo Sankei Nishiki Muroka Nama Genshu for my first drink. 800 yen.  
It came in a tokkuri, but the serving looked a little short of one go (180 ml).  
The first course arrived with the sake: an assortment of seasonal Shinshu pickled vegetables, rolled omelet, and other small dishes.  
The ingredients were fresh, the seasoning was just right, and everything was delicious. It worked well as an accompaniment to the sake too.  
As a muroka nama genshu, it had a pleasant balance of fizz, acidity, and sweetness.  
Its clean, refreshing character suited the style and went well with the food.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221233544725.webp" alt="image-20250221233544725" />  
  
Tempura came next. The batter was just right, making it delicious.  
Unusually, it came with a powder made from aged miso for dipping. Its gentle saltiness reminded me of flavored salt.  
The pairing was Kame no Umi Junmai Ginjo Nama Genshu, 780 yen.  
As expected of an unpasteurized sake, its acidity and sweetness stood out. The pairing with the tempura was a little less convincing, though.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221233609816.webp" alt="image-20250221233609816" />  
  
Finally, the main course: soba.  
There were two kinds—thick-cut dark soba and thin-cut white soba—served with tsuyu, fresh wasabi to grate, and sesame salt.  
The noodles were chewy and good, but the previous menu items tasted better.  
Still, it was refreshing as it was my first time grinding wasabi on a grater.  
For pairing, Kurouemon Sankei Nishiki Chikagumi Namagenshu. 800 yen  
It was a gunshu-style sake, with rustic rice notes that weren't unpleasant.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221233625799.webp" alt="image-20250221233625799" />  
  
The last course was mizugashi, dessert, with yokan and other sweets.  
It was okay to just cleanse your mouth.  
For the final pairing, a glass of Daishinshu junmai daiginjo inavikari. 1200 yen.  
This one was served by the glass. As expected of a junmai daiginjo, it was expensive and the serving was small.  
Sake has a clean taste, but you can still feel the sweetness and aroma of rice in moderation. It was a drink that seemed better suited as a mid-meal drink rather than dessert.  
  
Overall, the course was very satisfying for 3,000 yen, and there was plenty of delicious sake. This place certainly deserves to be called a great restaurant.  
This soba restaurant has a nice atmosphere, making it a good place to treat friends to lunch.  
This is a store I want to visit again when I come to Matsumoto City.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221233639159.webp" alt="image-20250221233639159" />  
  
After lunch, I moved to my next destination, Suwa City.  
Suwa Taisha, the representative attraction of Suwa City, is divided into upper and lower shrines, but due to time constraints, it was impossible to visit them all, so I decided to go to the main shrine of the upper shrines, which is the highest.  
To get there, I had to take the train from Matsumoto Station to Kami Suwa Station and then take a bus. Since I had to get off at Kami Suwa Station, I decided to visit the lake park along the way.  
  
When I got off at Kami Suwa Station, there was a foot bath right near the boarding area.  
These facilities are usually located outside the ticket gate, but it was a bit surprising to see them right after getting off the subway.  
I saw a few people using it...  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221233653332.webp" alt="image-20250221233653332" />  
  
There is a tourist information center right near Kami Suwa Station, so I stopped by.  
The photo below shows Suwa Hime, a local character from Suwa City.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221233732114.webp" alt="image-20250221233732114" />  
  
Shinshu's local sake was also on display. I was able to check out a variety of Sakagura, including Daishinshu, Kame no Umi, and Mikotsuru, which I had tried earlier.  
It's a pity that I can't taste everything...  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221233719722.webp" alt="image-20250221233719722" />  
  
Suwa was also famous as a hot springs area, so it also had an Onsen Musume character.  
Unusually, badges of other Onsen Musume characters were also displayed here, but unfortunately only goods featuring the local character, Kamisuwa Shizune, were for sale.  
As a souvenir, I purchased a 300 yen can badge and headed to Suwa City Lakeside Park.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221233705902.webp" alt="image-20250221233705902" />  
  
I found out later that Lake Suwa is also one of the sacred places in your name, and the large lake that appears in the story is Lake Suwa.  
It was vast and had ebb and flow, so it felt like the sea.  
It was very windy and quite cold, so I took a walk around the area for about 20 minutes.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221233828330.webp" alt="image-20250221233828330" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221233934825.webp" alt="image-20250221233934825" />  
  
<VideoLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/20241211_063749000_iOS.MOV" />  
  
Next, I arrived at Suwa Taisha Kamisha Honmiya by bus.  
The large torii gate at the entrance was impressive.  
When I arrived, it was around 4:50 PM, and there was no one there because it was time to finish the shrine's business, and the shrine office seemed busy with finishing touches.  
As it is an important shrine in Japan's Shinto history, the scenery within the shrine appeared to be neatly and well maintained, and the pilgrimage route was also well organized.  
It was smaller than I had expected from the name 'Taisha,' but that seemed understandable considering the shrine is divided among four sites.  
  
It is known as a pilgrimage spot for fans of ''Touhou: Mountain of Faith'' and ''The Elusive Samurai,'' but I saw no signs of either franchise.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221234056742.webp" alt="image-20250221234056742" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221234037993.webp" alt="image-20250221234037993" />  
  
Since I came to the shrine, I had some omikuji. I pulled out Obsidian Mikuji on the right.  
Don't worry about the results...  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250222005542191.webp" alt="image-20250222005542191" />  
  
Looking at the main street outside the shrine, I saw a commercial district centered around the shrine.  
Unfortunately, they were all closed so I couldn't tour the stores.  
I regret coming a little earlier...  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221234146702.webp" alt="image-20250221234146702" />  
  
There was also a Suwahime vending machine on the street.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221234109003.webp" alt="image-20250221234109003" />  
  
After this somewhat disappointing Suwa tour, I decided to return to Matsumoto and have dinner.  
The place I visited was the ramen restaurant I had my eye on, “Mensho Sakura.”  
It seemed to be a famous miso ramen restaurant in Matsumoto.  
There was a waiting sign outside to see if there was a frequent wait, but when I visited in the evening, I was able to get in right away without having to wait.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221234158751.webp" alt="image-20250221234158751" />  
  
What I ordered was the special Sakura miso ramen with rice, an additional egg, and Shinshu miso gyoza, for a total of 2,040 yen.  
Miso ramen has a very rich taste.  
Some Korean reviews said it was too salty to eat, but to me it was about as salty as a typical miso ramen, and tasted perfectly decent.  
A perfectly decent bowl of ramen. I wouldn't call it exceptional, but it was a reasonably good place.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250221234240246.webp" alt="image-20250221234240246" />  
  
After having dinner, I took a walk to Matsumoto Castle to see the Matsumoto Castle lit up at night, which I had seen at the tourist information center.  
Several videos were shown using the castle tower of Matsumoto Castle as a drawing board, and the quality was considerable.  
I have also seen the light-up at Nijo Castle in Kyoto, but it is even better than that...  
And the media art that utilized the shape of Matsumoto Castle's keep was impressive.  
  
The day ends with the lighting up of Matsumoto Castle.
