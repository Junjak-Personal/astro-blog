## Journal  
  
I woke up around 8 o'clock, got ready, and left the accommodation.  
A photo of the view of Amanohashidate directly from the window of the room I stayed in.  
The morning sunlight was so strong that the picture did not come out well.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107023710146.webp" alt="image-20250107023710146" />  
  
To go to the next destination, Tsuruga City, I first left the accommodation and took a 600 yen one-way motorboat to Amanohashidate Station.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107023332140.webp" alt="image-20250107023332140" />  
  
The route to Tsuruga is a bit complicated; you need to transfer three times in the following order: Amanohashidate Station -> Nishi-Maizuru Station -> Higashi-Maizuru Station -> Tsuruga Station.  
Since Kyoto Tango Railway runs up to Nishi-Maizuru Station, I couldn't go with the JR pass, so I bought a ticket for 650 yen.  
  
A Taiwanese man outside the station wanted to get to Kyoto and asked me for help. I checked Google Maps and explained the route.  
All we had to do was go to Nishi-Maizuru Station, so we talked about various things through a translator on the way.  
(The translator communicated in Chinese ↔ Japanese... )  
He is 58 years old and said that his dream was to travel to Japan alone and that he came this time to achieve that.  
At Nishi-Maizuru, he needed to head in the opposite direction toward Ayabe, so we parted ways.  
I silently hoped he'd make it to his destination without any trouble...  
  
Below, there was a section on the way where the Tango Railway crossed the Yura River, so I recorded it as a video.  
  
### Tsuruga  
  
Arrived at Tsuruga Station around 1:20.  
It was written that the Shinkansen connecting Tsuruga Station and Kanazawa Station opened on March 16th of this year.  
Perhaps that's why Tsuruga Station and the bus stop had a newly renovated feel.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107024941224.webp" alt="image-20250107024941224" />  
  
I also spotted what looked like a themed cafe or maid cafe. I'd never seen one inside a city's main station before, which was a surprise.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107031500135.webp" alt="image-20250107031500135" />  
  
There were many sculptures related to Galaxy Express 999 around Tsuruga Station, and it is said that they were built under the supervision of the original author, Leiji Matsumoto, to commemorate the 100th anniversary of the opening of Tsuruga Port in 1999.  
The original author is from Kitakyushu and has no significant relationship with Tsuruga, but it is said that the image of Tsuruga City includes ''Science City'', ''Port'', and ''Station'', so the Galaxy Express 999, which is related to the image, was adopted. Isn't this effort to revitalize local tourism resources worthy of imitation...  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107025246157.webp" alt="image-20250107025246157" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107025739350.webp" alt="image-20250107025739350" />  
  
On the way to Kehi Jingu, I stopped for lunch at Echizen Soba Mugiya.  
https://maps.app.goo.gl/VcLBWKaN2PGwyvVXA  
The menu I ordered was Tempura Udon, 850 yen. The taste was ordinary udon.  
It didn't have a very good rating of 3.6 on Google Maps, and I'm not sure why. The customer service was also good...  
Personally, about 4.0 points? I think so.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107025924957.webp" alt="image-20250107025924957" />  
  
After lunch, I arrived at Kehi Jingu.  
Founded in 702, Kehi Jingu is known as the most prestigious ichinomiya shrine of the former Echizen Province, around today's Tsuruga and Fukui. Its main deity is Isasawake-no-Okami, said to be a deified prince of Silla named Cheonilchang...  
Japan's links with Baekje are well known, so I was surprised to find a connection with Silla too.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107030328985.webp" alt="image-20250107030328985" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107030755894.webp" alt="image-20250107030755894" />  
  
As one of the few shrines with both ichinomiya and jingu status, it was quite large and seemed particularly well cared for.  
The ginkgo tree inside was also pretty cool...  
I paid my respects and drew a koi-themed omikuji. Small luck...

### Fukui  
  
I arrived at Fukui Station around 3:30 p.m. The first thing I saw after leaving the ticket gates was a life-size standee from the light novel Chitose Is in the Ramune Bottle.  
After some research, it seemed that the setting was Fukui Prefecture, and that it had been decided to become an animation in 2025, so it was installed to promote and revitalize tourism.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107032412025.webp" alt="image-20250107032412025" />  
  
Fukui Station was also clean and had a strong feeling that it had been recently renovated.  
The first thing you see as soon as you come out is a dinosaur.  
Fukui Prefecture is famous for its dinosaur fossils, so there were quite a few dinosaur-related sculptures around the tourist information center and station.  
I was quite interested when I was young, but I don't have much now so I'll pass.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107032601235.webp" alt="image-20250107032601235" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107032743581.webp" alt="image-20250107032743581" />

First, I headed to Fukui Guesthouse Sammie's, where I'd be staying, to check in.  
The distance from the station is about 5 minutes on foot? It is located close by.  
The inside of the guesthouse feels like this.  
When I entered, I was greeted by the owner and her daughter. It's a really close-knit family that makes me feel warm when I see them.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107033112380.webp" alt="image-20250107033112380" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107033131274.webp" alt="image-20250107033131274" />  
  
I asked about the red container in the photo below. Apparently, you fill it with hot water and put it under your bedding to warm it up...  
I've been traveling to Japan a lot and it was a bit surprising seeing it for the first time.  
Although it was an old house that was remodeled into a guest house, it wasn't that cold inside so I didn't use it.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107033200610.webp" alt="image-20250107033200610" />  
  
After asking the owner for sightseeing spots still worth visiting at this hour and restaurant recommendations, I headed toward the Asuwa Park area.  
As the sun was going to set in a little while, I headed to the bicycle rental shop shown in the picture, thinking about riding a bicycle all the way there, but strangely enough, the IC card payment did not work, so I wasted time...  
It was getting late, so the only places I could go were shrines that were open 24 hours a day, so I ended up visiting four shrines in Fukui today.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107033611290.webp" alt="image-20250107033611290" />  
  
There was Shibata Shrine on the way, so I visited it.  
According to the photo caption below, it is a shrine that worships the famous Shibata Katsuie and his wife Oichi during the Warring States Period.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107033748552.webp" alt="image-20250107033748552" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107033802743.webp" alt="image-20250107033802743" />  
  
After Oda Nobunaga's death, Shibata Katsuie refused to accept Hashiba Hideyoshi's authority. Following his defeat at the Battle of Shizugatake, he died with his wife Oichi in their burning castle.  
The eldest of Oichi's three daughters had the strange fate of becoming Toyotomi Hideyoshi's concubine...  
  
I'll stop talking about history here, and I picked up an omikuji because it was available.  
There were lots of unusual fortunes. I tried the danmikuji, a men's omikuji...?  
Small luck again...  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107034202188.webp" alt="image-20250107034202188" />  
  
Leaving Shibata Shrine behind, I walked to Atagozaka, which is the entrance to Asuwa Park.  
I'd decided to visit after reading a Fukui travelogue on brunch. With houses lining the steps, it was a hillside street with a lovely atmosphere.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107034313443.webp" alt="image-20250107034313443" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107034355953.webp" alt="image-20250107034355953" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107034510176.webp" alt="image-20250107034510176" />  
  
By the time I climbed the steps and reached Asuwa Shrine, its office had already closed.  
Unfortunately, I gave up on Omikuji.  
Over 1,500 years old, Asuwa Shrine is said to be the oldest shrine in Echizen, enshrining Emperor Keitai (継体).  
The shrine site is not that large, but is it a medium-sized shrine in the area?  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107034706416.webp" alt="image-20250107034706416" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107034632478.webp" alt="image-20250107034632478" />  
  
After Asuwa Shrine, I headed to Keya Kurotatsu Shrine, recommended by the guesthouse owner.  
It was below Asuwa Park, about an eight-minute walk away.  
I photographed an Asuwa Park information sign along the way.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107035213866.webp" alt="image-20250107035213866" />  
  
This black dragon is Keya Kurotatsu (毛谷黒龍), a guardian deity said to live in the nearby Kuzu River and be much loved by local people.  
Keya Kurotatsu Shrine is the ancient shrine dedicated to this deity.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107035417513.webp" alt="image-20250107035417513" />  
  
Known as a power spot to ward off evil spirits, there were devices within the shrine to ward off bad luck.  
One of them is Yakuwariishi. As shown in the video, it is said that bad luck is driven away by throwing a plate at a stone and breaking it.  
The usage fee is 100 yen.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107035823851.webp" alt="image-20250107035823851" />  
  
  
Next up was Negaikakeishi. It is said that all you have to do is think of what you wish for and tap on the stone three times. <ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107040118335.webp" alt="image-20250107040118335" />  
  
My next stop was Sakaenoyashiro Shrine.  
It is famous for having 100,000 cranes folded and hung in the main shrine, and it is also known as Echizen Toshogu Shrine because it enshrines Tokugawa Ieyasu as its main shrine...  
I don't know if 100,000 cranes is real, but it was a lot anyway.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107040640335.webp" alt="image-20250107040640335" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107040447787.webp" alt="image-20250107040447787" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107040652385.webp" alt="image-20250107040652385" />  
  
Somehow the afternoon had turned into a shrine tour. That was enough shrines for today, so I headed to Yoroppaken's main branch for dinner on the guesthouse owner's recommendation.  
I've seen this in Tsuruga City too, and it's a famous sosukatsudon restaurant in the area.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107041041110.webp" alt="image-20250107041041110" />  
  
Yoroppaken was celebrating an impressive 111 years since its founding. It started in Tokyo, but after the shop was damaged in the Great Kanto Earthquake, the owner moved to Fukui and opened here in 1924.  
So, the main store in Fukui is interesting because it can be considered a 100-year-old building.  
It seems to be a specialty in the region…  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107041136795.webp" alt="image-20250107041136795" />  
  
There was a beer on the menu with the nickname Queen of Germany, so I tried it.  
When I ordered beer, I received 5 edamame as a free snack.  
The taste is an ordinary dark beer. I don't really like dark beer, but I felt like I ordered it for no reason.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107041315609.webp" alt="image-20250107041315609" />  
  
Next came the sauce katsudon.  
I ordered the four-cutlet set: pork, fried shrimp, minced meat, and beef.  
Unlike ordinary katsudon topped with egg, spring onion, and onion, this was simply sauce-coated cutlets on rice.  
The sauce seemed to be of the Worcestershire variety and did not suit my taste. The menchi katsu was the best.  
A total of 3,100 yen came out like this.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250107041524284.webp" alt="image-20250107041524284" />  
  
It was a shame to end the evening like this, so I looked for a nearby bar to go to.  
I happened to find Rue Club, an old bar with good Google Maps reviews, and headed over.  
 https://maps.app.goo.gl/MyyLP9ngzMrLxnY59  
  
This bar is run by an old couple. Drinks are usually priced around 900 yen.  
The first drink was a gin-based cocktail, but I forgot the name. All I remember is that it wasn't bad.  
After that, I had the following drinks.  
  
- Balalaika  
- A shot of Nikka Miyagikyo  
- Salty Dog  
- Fukui craft beer (a gift from the bartender)  
- Tequila Ginger  
- Glen Parker Malt on the rocks (the woman sitting to my right bought it for me)  
- Two glasses of umeshu, one bought for me by the man on my left. The bartender had made the plum liqueur himself; it was very tasty despite its strength.  
  
It was a shame that I couldn't take a good picture because I was too busy talking.  
  
For snacks, the owner's wife served homemade oden, nuts, and something with salami on cucumber whose name I forgot to ask.  
The cocktails and alcohol were decent.  
It was really nice to be able to have various conversations with the master and his wife rather than drinking here.  
As in Okayama, I'm not sure why conversations with older people tend to go this way, but we had an almost debate-like discussion of Korean and Japanese history, the past, the present, and the future—both countries seeming to compete in heading in the wrong direction.  
 (For reference, the master said he visited Korea about 40 years ago. He said he was in his 70s.)  
  
I talked about the above topic with the man who came in the middle.  
He seemed to be a regular, bantering with the bartender like a manzai comedy duo. The bartender played the fool, the boke, and the customer the straight man, the tsukkomi. Their chemistry was fun to watch.  
Later, a woman with two children in their twenties came in. I mostly talked with her about travel.  
(She said she'd traveled to Korea before.)  
  
Overall, I think I had a really worthwhile time as I was able to talk about various topics.  
The bartender was still young at heart and suggested funny ideas like finding gold with a metal detector to make money. That helped make it such an enjoyable evening.  
Eventually, I left at the same time as the bartender finished work. I'd spent about three and a half hours there.  
The bill was 9,000 yen. I was grateful to the man and the woman for buying me drinks too.  
Next time I go on a trip to Fukui, I plan to come again.  
The couple's local dialect was quite challenging to understand, though.

Having drunk quite a lot, I took a walk to the Fukui Castle Ruins Park to work off dinner and sober up.  
The Fukui Castle ruins are really castle ruins. The only traces of the castle were a moat and some castle gates.  
The inside of the castle was used as a major government office, including the Fukui Prefectural Office... Still, it is a nice park for a walk.
