## Journal  
  
### Kanazawa  
  
Today started with breakfast at the hotel again.  
The menu is almost the same.  
I'd originally planned to go straight to Toyama and visit the Glass Art Museum and Fugan Canal Kansui Park,  
I learned from a pamphlet that Omicho Market is one of the famous attractions in Kanazawa, so I changed my plan to visit Omicho Market.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250109222537204.webp" alt="image-20250109222537204" />  
  
I walked through rainy Nagamachi toward Omicho Market.

I thought king crab was the most expensive, but I was surprised that there were more expensive crabs.

###  Toyama  
  
After briefly looking around Omicho Market, I took the Shinkansen from Kanazawa Station to Toyama Station.  
When I came out of the platform, a sushi model greeted me. Toyama sushi seems to be famous.  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110000706310.webp" alt="image-20250110000706310" />  
  
I took a short bus and arrived at the Toyama City Glass Museum. The bus fare is 210 yen, which is a bit expensive.  
Unfortunately, I left my umbrella on the bus, but I gave up on it and went in first.  
The building was showing off its unique grandeur.  
Admission itself is free, but like the art museum I went to yesterday, admission to special exhibitions (including permanent ones) costs about 1,200 yen.  
Admission to the permanent exhibition hall is 200 yen.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110000949172.webp" alt="image-20250110000949172" />  
  
The interior of the building is a combination of wood and glass, creating a sense of sophistication and stability at the same time.  
The space was open all the way up to the sixth floor, giving it an airy feel. I was impressed by the architect's work.  
The 3rd, 4th, and 5th floors were probably a library, so there were quite a few customers using the library.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110001421863.webp" alt="image-20250110001421863" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110001447938.webp" alt="image-20250110001447938" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110001634202.webp" alt="image-20250110001634202" />  
  
The permanent exhibition hall is on the 6th floor, where you can see the glass crafts of ''Dale Chihuly''.  
The warm-looking guy below...  
I had never had the opportunity to see such complex and elaborate glass crafts in person, so I watched it in amazement.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110002150407.webp" alt="image-20250110002150407" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110002300321.webp" alt="image-20250110002300321" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110002327120.webp" alt="image-20250110002327120" />  
  
The fourth and fifth floors held a special exhibition, "Paris of Longing," about the work and life of the famous glass artist Émile Gallé—whom I'd never heard of before.  
As someone who knows very little about art, I preferred the more flamboyant work by the friendly-looking man above.  
  
The growth and death of a man named Émile Gallé are explained along with his glass artifacts. As expected, the Japanese language used in the museum is difficult, so I used the power of a translator to mainly appreciate a few works of interest.  
Most of the crafts were centered around bowls and cups, but as time went on, more and more works depicting insects appeared.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110002815233.webp" alt="image-20250110002815233" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110002750955.webp" alt="image-20250110002750955" />  
  
After looking around the special exhibition hall, I went down to the second floor and stopped by the souvenir shop.  
The shop sold all sorts of glassware, including glass ochoko sake cups like those below. I bought one that caught my eye.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110003306662.webp" alt="image-20250110003306662" />  
  
I needed to stop by Toyama Station to reach the next destination, Canal Park, so I took the tram to Toyama Station.  
The weather was cloudy and it kept raining on and off. It's such a shame that I lost my umbrella.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110003503247.webp" alt="image-20250110003503247" />  
  
I briefly stopped at a souvenir shop in Toyama Station and noticed Masuizumi for sale.  
Souvenir shops tend to focus on local sake. Until then, I hadn't realized that Masuizumi, one of my favorite brands, came from Toyama Prefecture...  
I discovered that the Masuizumi brewery was in Iwase, north of Toyama Station, and immediately changed my plans to visit.  
Before going, I called the bus center to find my lost umbrella, but they said nothing had arrived yet...  
I really liked this umbrella, but I put my regrets aside and took the tram to Iwase.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110003612519.webp" alt="image-20250110003612519" />  
  
The streetcar platform at Toyama Station was very serious about streetcars, with 8 platforms.  
Platform 5 towards Iwase.  
When riding the Toyama tram, Google Maps was not helpful, so I had to check the direction myself and ride it.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110004510470.webp" alt="image-20250110004510470" />  
  
Google Maps had some odd information about Toyama's trams: Higashi-Iwase was labeled Higashi-Iwata, for example. It works perfectly well even in places much more rural than this...  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110004725515.webp" alt="image-20250110004725515" />  
  
After about 20 minutes, I arrived at Higashi-Iwase.  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110004705530.webp" alt="image-20250110004705530" />  
  
As soon as I got off, the rain started falling steadily again.  
Since I don't have an umbrella, I have to wear waterproof outerwear.  
  
I walked to Masuizumi Brewery but couldn't find anything resembling a shop selling its sake.  
If you look at the Google reviews, they say they tasted it and did various things...  
I was thinking of going inside and asking, but everyone seemed busy so I gave up.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110005337114.webp" alt="image-20250110005337114" />  
  
Instead, I decided to look around and find a place that seemed like it.  
Iwase's streets were lined with well-maintained traditional houses. The streets and scenery were lovely.  
It's surprising why there aren't many tourists. There are so many great tourist attractions in Kanazawa that I thought that Toyama next door might not be as popular.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110005515927.webp" alt="image-20250110005515927" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110005310080.webp" alt="image-20250110005310080" />  
  
After exploring for a while, I found the Former Baba Residence, now a museum, and decided to go in.  
Admission was only 100 yen. I'd visited several old Japanese homes by this point, but the Baba Residence was astonishingly spacious compared with the others. Its garden was beautifully maintained too...  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110005757446.webp" alt="image-20250110005757446" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110005812204.webp" alt="image-20250110005812204" />  
  
After finishing the sightseeing, it was still raining, so I was taking shelter from the rain in front of the mansion, and suddenly I saw a double rainbow floating around.  
Was it trying to cheer me up after getting rained on?  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110005916558.webp" alt="image-20250110005916558" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110010005713.webp" alt="image-20250110010005713" />  
  
When the rain stopped, I kept exploring and went into a shop that looked like a brewery sales outlet. They pointed me toward Masuizumi's tasting room and shops, as shown below.  
It turned out to be only another two minutes' walk from the Baba Residence...  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110010104026.webp" alt="image-20250110010104026" />  
  
According to Google Maps, the name is Saseki. https://maps.app.goo.gl/e4Rxhqfnkk4LFgPr7  
Google Maps listed it as a bar, so I'd assumed it was an izakaya. In fact, it was a place for tasting Masuizumi and buying related products...  
Saseki, with Masuizumi prominently written outside.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110010410183.webp" alt="image-20250110010410183" />  
  
The inside of the building was quite spacious, and there were various exhibits on display, as well as a refrigerator with various goods, snacks, and alcohol on display.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110010720545.webp" alt="image-20250110010720545" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110010734048.webp" alt="image-20250110010734048" />  
  
When asked about tasting, there are three types in total.  
  
1. Pay 200-500 yen per glass for tasting  
2. All-you-can-drink tasting: 15 minutes for 1,000 yen  
3. All-you-can-drink tasting: 30 minutes for 2,000 yen  
  
The all-you-can-drink option was limited to once a day.  
After debating between options 2 and 3, I decided on option 3.  
I also ordered snacks for 300 yen. Types include beef jerky, dried hotaruika, and pickled mackerel.  
I also bought a 500 ml bottle of water for 100 yen, to drink between tastings and rinse my glass.  
They gave me a 30-minute timer and said to start it after taking out and pouring my first sake.  
  
I could taste every bottle in the refrigerator I'd seen earlier. Anyone who loves sake, especially Masuizumi, would go wild for this tasting.  
There were even quite a few aged sake varieties matured for 30 years. The all-you-can-drink option was ridiculously good value.  
(I searched on Google and found that a bottle of 720 ml costs about 30,000 yen?)  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110010935438.webp" alt="image-20250110010935438" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110010946222.webp" alt="image-20250110010946222" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110011108958.webp" alt="image-20250110011108958" />  
  
I started with an aged junmai daiginjo brewed in Heisei 6, or 1994.  
As you'd expect of an aged sake, it had a pale orange color and a rich, lovely flavor. A pity these aged bottles weren't for sale...  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110011328302.webp" alt="image-20250110011328302" />  
  
The second glass is a limited edition Daiginjo, Koshu brewed in 2007.  
It had a little more alcoholic bite than the 1994 one, with the character of an aged sake.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110011547563.webp" alt="image-20250110011547563" />  
  
For drinks three through six, I chose Masuizumi's kijoshu varieties, which I absolutely love.  
The third glass was Kijoshu × Kijoshu. Kijoshu is made using sake in place of brewing water. This one apparently used kijoshu itself as the brewing liquid, hence the name.  
The taste was very sweet and had a rich caramel flavor. Of course it was delicious.  
I feel like I don’t need any other snacks. If I had the chance, I would buy it or try it again.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110012135192.webp" alt="image-20250110012135192" />  
  
The fourth glass is regular Kijoshu.  
Compared with Kijoshu × Kijoshu, it was less intensely flavored and less sweet.  
Kijoshu × Kijoshu was so richly flavored that finding a suitable snack might be difficult,  
I feel like regular kijoshu would be better to eat with snacks. This was also delicious.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110012249039.webp" alt="image-20250110012249039" />  
  
The fifth glass is Kijoshu Shibori.  
It was so sweet that I found it a little difficult to drink.  
I was surprised because it was my first time feeling this much sweetness in Japanese sake.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110012152049.webp" alt="image-20250110012152049" />  
  
The 6th glass is the long-awaited oak barrel-aged Kijoshu.  
I'd been amazed by this sake when it was served as a pairing at an omakase meal in Tokyo. Since I hadn't seen it in liquor shops, I'd been looking for it ever since.  
It's been a while since I tried it, and it's a drink that doesn't betray my expectations.  
The strengths of Kijoshu combined with the woody scent created an explosive synergy. Decided to buy it right away.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110012405949.webp" alt="image-20250110012405949" />  
  
The snacks I purchased for 300 yen ran out, so I bought additional dried hotaruika, which was delicious. The price is 748 yen  
  
The 7th glass is this store's limited original liquor with a unique and pretty bottle. Toyama Japan.  
The taste of Japanese sake was ordinary, and the bottle was pretty, so I thought it would be a good gift.  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110012501137.webp" alt="image-20250110012501137" />  
  
The 8th glass is Junmai Ginjo Shu, aged for 5 years at Arimine Dam.  
I didn’t quite understand what it meant to be aged in a dam for 5 years.  
The flavor was good as it was aged for 5 years. I think aged Japanese sake is good, too.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110012528187.webp" alt="image-20250110012528187" />  
  
The 9th cup is Doyu no Namazake.  
The label said do you know , making a play on words… .  
I looked it up: a junmai daiginjo made with Isehikari rice grown on Toyuno's terraced paddies and the brewery's own yeast.  
Japanese sake with a sweet taste and a sour and carbonated taste unique to Namazake. It was okay.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110012550633.webp" alt="image-20250110012550633" />  
  
The 10th cup is Bo Shibori, I don’t know what Bo Shibori is…  
I meant to ask after I finished drinking, but I forgot.  
I couldn't find anything different about it, it was just ordinary delicious Japanese sake.  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110012634800.webp" alt="image-20250110012634800" />  
  
The 11th glass is R2 Japanese sake made with wine yeast.  
I wondered if there was something different about using wine yeast, but I didn’t notice a significant difference in taste…  
I guess it's because I'm a little drunk at this point...  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110013154495.webp" alt="image-20250110013154495" />  
  
The twelfth glass was Henri Giraud × Masuizumi.  
At around 9,900 yen, it's one of the more expensive bottles in Masuizumi's range.  
From what I've tried, it doesn't seem like it's worth the money..? Of course it wasn't bad.  
It is said that champagne aged oak barrels were used, but the oak barrel flavor did not seem to be evident for some reason.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110013244412.webp" alt="image-20250110013244412" />  
  
The 13th glass is Daiginjo Kotobuki aged for over 30 years.  
I couldn't taste much difference from the 30-year-old junmai daiginjo aged sake I'd tried earlier. Still, it was good.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110013307819.webp" alt="image-20250110013307819" />  
  
The 14th glass is Junmai Daiginjo Kotobuki Platinum.  
Masuizumi's daiginjo and junmai daiginjo usually use rice polished to 50%, but this one used Yamada Nishiki polished to 35%. It had the clean flavor of junmai daiginjo.  
This bottle cost 11,000 yen...  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110013425825.webp" alt="image-20250110013425825" />  
  
The 15th and final cup is Shinshu No. 1 Shibori Namazake.  
Combination of namazake + nigori. It wasn't bad. The feeling of high-quality makgeolli  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110013439883.webp" alt="image-20250110013439883" />  
  
Overall, all Japanese sakes were delicious, but I felt that Kijoshu was the most delicious.  
I immediately purchased oak barrel-aged Kijo-shu for 4,950 yen.  
I bought a small ichigo-masu wooden cup as a souvenir for 300 yen—it doesn't hold a full go. I also bought more of the dried firefly squid I'd enjoyed, for 748 yen.  
  
After the Masuizumi tasting, it felt a shame to leave Iwase without seeing more. I decided to walk to Iwase Beach.  
The scenery had the feel of a quiet rural port or beachside village. Sunset made it even more atmospheric.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110013754645.webp" alt="image-20250110013754645" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110013821763.webp" alt="image-20250110013821763" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110013907094.webp" alt="image-20250110013907094" />

<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110014023365.webp" alt="image-20250110014023365" />

Google Maps said to go to Higashi-Iwase Station, so I walked about 3 km and arrived at the station.  
I found out later that I could walk a little less if I took the streetcar from Iwasehama Station.  
I can't trust Google Maps in Toyama...  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110014045042.webp" alt="image-20250110014045042" />  
  
Take the street train and return to Toyama Station.  
I was thinking about eating crab in Kanazawa or Toyama for dinner.  
I decided on Toyama's specialty, black shoyu ramen, and headed to Ramen Isshin's Toyama Station main branch.  
https://maps.app.goo.gl/7TcgkeSKhrJA8Czz7  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110014444110.webp" alt="image-20250110014444110" />  
  
Since it is a famous place in Toyama, I thought there would be a wait, but fortunately there wasn't.  
I went straight in and ordered the special black shoyu ramen, a small rice, and rare chashu.  
The fact that the sign says ‘no chemical seasoning added’ and ‘home-made noodles’ is something I realized later after looking at the photo.  
  
When the food arrived, I immediately put the rare chashu into the broth to cook it a little more.  
I had the soup, and it was delicious, deep and full of flavor.  
I usually like tonkotsu rather than shoyu ramen, but this one tastes really good.  
It was the best shoyu ramen I'd had in Japan, and one of my favorite bowls of ramen overall.  
Red pepper powder and pepper powder were prepared as toppings, so I added them and it suited my taste better.  
I even added the rice to the broth and finished every last drop.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110014734963.webp" alt="image-20250110014734963" />  
  
It was written that “Koji Murofushi,” a famous Japanese hammer thrower, even ate kaedama here.  
I also sat and ate here. Haha  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250110014839053.webp" alt="image-20250110014839053" />  
  
Return by Shinkansen from Toyama to Kanazawa.  
I think I walked back to the hotel...?  
On the way, I stopped at a convenience store for Dekavita and Famichiki, then at a wagashi shop for a pumpkin sweet,  
before heading back inside.
