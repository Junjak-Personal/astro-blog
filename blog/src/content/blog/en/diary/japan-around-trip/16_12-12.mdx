## Travel Diary  
  
This morning's schedule is Daio Wasabi Farm.  
To get to the wasabi farm from Matsuyama, you had to take the train to Hotaka Station, but the intervals between trains were quite sparse, so you had to be careful about timing.  
My next accommodation was in Nagano, so I left my luggage at the coin locker and waited for the train.  
I'm glad the weather is nice today.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250222215327114.webp" alt="image-20250222215327114" />  
  
I arrived at Hotaka Station like that, I think it took about 20 minutes.  
The area around Hotaka Station is not that busy, and the surrounding buildings are low, so the Japanese Alps are clearly visible.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250222215708844.webp" alt="image-20250222215708844" />  
  
There are no buses running to Daio Wasabi Farm, so transportation options are limited, but when I looked at the Google review of Daio Wasabi Farm, I saw that there was a bicycle rental shop near the station, so I decided to ride a bicycle.  
(Bike/walk/taxi; walking takes about an hour, so not recommended.)  
  
It was a rental store called Shinano An, and the usage fee was cheap, around 200 yen for an hour for a regular bicycle and 300 yen for an hour for an electric bicycle.  
He must have been used to tourists riding bicycles to Daio Farm, so he drew a recommended route to the farm on a map and gave it to me.  
The red sign on the left side of the photo is Shinano.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250222224320740.webp" alt="image-20250222224320740" />  
  
Before visiting Daio Farm, I decided to first visit nearby Hotaka Shrine, a prominent shrine in the area.  
This is what the entrance and interior of the scriptures look like.  
There were many tall coniferous trees around the shrine, giving the feeling of being in nature.  
There were quite a few locals who visited the area to worship or take a walk.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250222220629239.webp" alt="image-20250222220629239" />  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250222220654530.webp" alt="image-20250222220654530" />  
  
Hotaka Shrine is one of the three major shrines in Shinano Province and is said to be known as the shrine of the Japanese Alps.  
A shrine that has been worshiped by local residents for a long time, with its history recorded in records dating back to 927 AD.  
The size of the shrine was quite large, and the interior of the shrine was neatly and well maintained.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250222220833205.webp" alt="image-20250222220833205" />  
  
If you go to a shrine, omikuji is a must-see.  
I chose the birthstone fortune labeled 'Sankaku Mikuji' on the far right. The result was chukichi, moderate good fortune.  
It wasn't bad at all.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250222220707160.webp" alt="image-20250222220707160" />  
  
There was also a sign with instructions on how to visit the shrine, and I quite liked it because it was translated into Korean and had a cute shaman character.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250222220713749.webp" alt="image-20250222220713749" />  
  
And next to Ema, I was able to see a Dojo god made of stainless steel.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250222221452661.webp" alt="image-20250222221452661" />  
  
After looking around Hotaka Shrine, I pedaled towards Daio Farm again.  
A drug store called American's Drug can be seen in the middle.  
It was interesting because it seemed like there was a lot of potential for misunderstanding if foreigners saw it.  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250222221840918.webp" alt="image-20250222221840918" />  
  
The road was designed mainly for cars, so getting there by bicycle or on foot wasn't easy.  
I had to keep checking to see if a car was coming, and if a car came, I had to get out of the way, so it took about 30 minutes, a little later than the estimated time on Google Maps.  
However, the road there had its own charm, and I thought the feeling of riding a bicycle along a long road with the Japanese Alps at your back was romantic.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250222221931177.webp" alt="image-20250222221931177" />  
  
After some difficulty, I arrived at Daio Wasabi Farm.  
It felt like they were generating tourism revenue by selling food and souvenirs inside, without charging an entrance fee.<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250222222235541.webp" alt="image-20250222222235541" />  
  
The farm wasn't especially large; I could cover all the walking routes in about an hour.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250222222351904.webp" alt="image-20250222222351904" />  
  
Before entering the farm, there were several shops selling wasabi products, and I was interested in wasabi ice cream, so I bought one.  
What I purchased was premium wasabi soft cream. The difference from regular wasabi is that the cone is more luxurious and has actual wasabi on the side.  
Wasabi ice cream + raw wasabi felt a bit excessive, so I thought it would be better to just eat regular wasabi ice cream.  
Still, it tastes pretty good, so I think it's okay to eat it at least once as a souvenir.

Tomorrow's plan was to go to Jigokudani Wild Monkey Park, so I left the hotel and went to Nagaden Nagano Station to purchase a Snow Monkey Pass.  
The Snow Monkey Pass can be purchased from a vending machine at Nagaden Nagano Station, and the two-day pass costs 4,000 yen. The composition is as follows.  
  
- Free one-time entry to Jigokudani Wild Monkey Park  
- Unlimited use of Nagano Dentetsu line local trains and limited express non-reserved seats for 2 days  
- Unlimited use of the Nagaden Express Bus for 2 days  
- Unlimited use of Nagaden regular bus for 2 days  
  
Here, the one-time entrance fee to the monkey park (800 yen) and the round-trip cost of the express bus to the monkey park (3,600 yen) alone exceed 4,000 yen, so it can be considered advantageous to purchase this pass.

Zenkoji arrived later.  
Zenkoji is a Buddhist temple built around the 7th century and is said to house the first Buddha statue brought when Buddhism was introduced from Baekje around the 6th century.  The Buddha statue is closed to the public.  
Unlike the various sects of Buddhism in Japan, the characteristic of Zenkoji is that it does not belong to a specific sect and accepts everyone without discrimination.  
The origin of the name is that the founder of Zenkoji is Honda Yoshimitsu, and it is called Zenkoji (Zenkwang Temple) after Yoshimitsu (義光).  
In addition to Nagano, where Zenkoji is the head office, there are Zenkojis all over the country, and as of 1992, there are about 443 of them.  
There were quite a few.  
  
The photo below shows Zenkoji's Sanmon, which is said to have been built in 1750.  
I looked it up later and found out that you can go up to the second floor to see the view, but I was a little disappointed that I couldn't go up there.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250222231048107.webp" alt="image-20250222231048107" />  
  
The photo below shows the main temple of Zenkoji.  
I saw many tourists taking pictures in front of the main temple.  
It was a magnificent size worthy of Zenkoji's reputation as an important place in Japanese Buddhist history.  
Fukui's Eiheiji Temple had a similar feel to Korean Buddhism because it was located in the mountains, but Zenkoji had a different feel as it was located in the center of Nagano City.  
It costs 600 yen to visit the inside, but since there was not much time left, I only bought an amulet from the main hall and came out.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250222231034165.webp" alt="image-20250222231034165" />  
  
The photo below shows the Mizuko Kyuku Avalokitesvara Bodhisattva on the west side of the Zenkoji main hall. I took a picture of the various items offered in the center that caught my eye.  
Mizuko refers to babies who died due to miscarriage, stillbirth, or abortion, and was located at Zenkoji as a bodhisattva in the Mizuko Buddhist faith to commemorate these babies and comfort their souls.  
Since it is Buddhism for the common people, it was impressive that worldly items were offered.  
  
<ImageLoader src="/files/blog/diary/japan-around-trip/assets/image-20250222231117931.webp" alt="image-20250222231117931" />
